The core point

A model is a fixed function: tokens go in, numbers flow through billions of weights, tokens come out. Training sets those weights once, slowly and at great cost. Inference runs the frozen weights on every request, and that is where latency and cost live. Embeddings take the model's internal numbers and use them directly as a measure of meaning you can store and search.

  1. Weeks 1–2 · LearningWhat is a model, and how does it learn?

    Tokens, loss and gradients, learned by building them rather than memorizing a glossary first.

  2. Weeks 3–4 · TransformersHow does a transformer turn text into predictions?

    Vectors and attention first, then you train a small GPT yourself.

  3. Weeks 5–6 · InferenceHow do frozen weights answer a request?

    Decoding, sampling, serving and cost. Your eval set starts here and is reused to the end.

  4. Weeks 7–8 · Retrieval & adaptationHow do I make a model work on my data?

    Embeddings and RAG first, fine-tuning last, in the order the decision ladder recommends.

The one idea to keep: weights change only during training. Everything you build on top of an API, including RAG, is inference. The decision ladder says prompt → retrieve → fine-tune → train: only move down a step when the one above fails. Weeks 5–8 now follow that ladder in order.

Self-check answers

Try each one yourself before opening it.

W1: Why can the same sentence cost more tokens in one language than another?

Tokenizers are built from training text that is mostly English, so common English words become single tokens while other languages and scripts get split into more pieces. You pay per token, and the context window is counted in tokens.

W2: Training loss keeps falling but validation loss starts rising. What is happening?

The model is overfitting: memorizing the training data instead of learning patterns that generalize. Keep the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss (early stopping), add data, shrink the model or add regularization.

W3: Attention and semantic search both use dot products. What is compared in each?

In attention, one token's query is compared with the other tokens' keys inside the model, to decide how much of each token's information to mix in. In semantic search, two finished text embeddings are compared to measure how close their meanings are. Cosine similarity is a dot product of normalized vectors. The math is the same, but the stage and purpose differ.

W4: Why is a freshly pretrained base model not yet a helpful assistant?

Pretraining only teaches it to continue text like its training data, so it might answer a question with more questions. Instruction tuning (SFT) and preference tuning (RLHF, DPO) teach it to follow instructions and reply as an assistant.

W5: Why can a model with a 128K context window still "forget" earlier chat?

The window is a hard limit, not a promise of perfect recall. Chat apps often trim or summarize old turns to save cost, so the text may not be in the window at all. Even when it is, models recall details buried in the middle of a long context less reliably than details near the start or end.

W6: How much GPU memory does a 7B model in 16-bit need for its weights?

7 billion parameters × 2 bytes ≈ 14 GB. The KV cache and activations need more on top. At 4-bit the weights alone drop to about 3.5 GB.

W7: Why is embedding a document inference, not training? What would training mean here?

The embedding model's weights stay frozen; you run a forward pass and read out a vector. Training here means contrastive training or fine-tuning of the embedding model itself on pairs such as (question, relevant passage), so related items land closer together.

W8: Why does fine-tuning teach style and format more reliably than new facts?

Style and format appear in every training example, so a few hundred examples push the weights consistently. A new fact appears only a few times and competes with what pretraining stored, and the model can't signal when it is unsure. Facts that change belong in retrieval, where you can update and cite them.
